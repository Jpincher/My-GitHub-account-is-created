Part 1 Planning and preparation

Lesson planning and preparation for students aged 15

Selected topic and rationale

The topic is Sticks: Exploring natural materials through scientific investigation. Students will examine how sticks differ in length, diameter, mass, texture, flexibility and condition, then investigate how these characteristics influence their suitability for different purposes.

Sticks are accessible, inexpensive natural resources that connect classroom learning with the environment. For 15-year-olds, the challenge extends beyond describing appearances: students will develop hypotheses, measure properties, control variables and evaluate evidence. A guiding question will be: What makes a stick suitable for a particular purpose, and how can we test our ideas?

Students could compare samples and recommend suitable sticks for a model structure, justifying their choices with experimental results. They will also consider responsible collection and the importance of fallen wood as habitat.

Target audience

The lessons are designed for a mixed-attainment group of 15-year-old secondary students. Learners are expected to have some experience of measuring, recording results and recognising variables, although their confidence and scientific vocabulary may vary.

Practical group work will provide opportunities for observation, discussion and shared problem-solving. Differentiation will include illustrated vocabulary, structured recording sheets, worked examples and clearly defined group roles. Extension tasks will challenge students to analyse relationships between measurements and evaluate competing explanations. Accessible, pre-collected samples and alternative recording methods will support learners who find outdoor collection or handling materials difficult.

Learning objectives and curriculum alignment

The provisional curriculum reference is England’s Key Stage 4 science programme, particularly Working scientifically. The topic provides a context for practising these skills; it is not a separately prescribed curriculum unit. Alignment should be adapted if another national curriculum or examination specification applies.

By the end of the lesson sequence, students will be able to demonstrate the following outcomes.

Selected generative AI tool and rationale

A teacher-operated text-generating model, such as ChatGPT, will support preparation of the lesson sequence. Its purpose is to help produce and refine teaching resources while the teacher retains responsibility for accuracy, suitability and assessment.

The model will support the following activities:

Planning: generating alternative investigation questions and sequencing activities from observation to evaluation.

Differentiation: adapting the same task into scaffolded, standard and extension versions while preserving the learning objectives.

Learning materials: drafting vocabulary lists, recording sheets, discussion prompts and examples of evidence-based conclusions.

Assessment preparation: suggesting diagnostic questions, exit tickets and rubric descriptors for teacher review.

A text-generating model is appropriate because these tasks require adaptable explanations, questions and structured documents. One model is sufficient for this planning stage.

All generated content will be checked before classroom use, particularly practical procedures, scientific explanations and assessment answers. Students will collect their own experimental data. Any AI-generated example data will be clearly labelled as simulated. Direct student accounts are unnecessary for the planned activities; any later student use will follow school policy and applicable age requirements.

Part 2 Unit outline and assessment approach

Unit outline and assessment approach

Topic introduction

A fallen stick may appear ordinary, but it offers an opportunity to investigate how natural materials behave. Why do some sticks bend while others snap? Does a thicker stick always resist bending more effectively? How could we choose the most suitable stick for a model structure?

Students will explore these questions through observation, measurement and practical investigation. They will compare characteristics including length, diameter, mass, surface texture, flexibility and condition. They will distinguish between observations, predictions and conclusions, using evidence to explain their findings.

The topic also introduces responsible use of natural resources. Students will consider how fallen wood supports habitats and why collecting materials should minimise environmental disturbance.

Unit description

This unit is designed for mixed-attainment students aged 15 and comprises four lessons of approximately 60 minutes. It develops scientific enquiry through the investigation of a familiar natural material.

Students move from describing and measuring stick samples to planning an investigation, collecting data and evaluating findings. Their final task is to recommend suitable sticks for a small model structure, supporting their choices with evidence.

The unit provisionally aligns with England’s Key Stage 4 science emphasis on working scientifically: developing hypotheses, identifying variables, taking measurements, analysing data and evaluating methods.

Teacher-operated generative AI will support the preparation of differentiated worksheets, questioning prompts and assessment resources. The teacher will check all generated materials before use. Students’ conclusions will be based on their own observations and measurements.

Assessment approach

Assessment will combine initial questioning, feedback during learning and an individual final task. Success criteria will be shared before practical work.

Diagnostic assessment

At the start of Lesson 1, students will describe two contrasting sticks and respond to the question: Which would bend more under the same load, and why? Responses will reveal existing vocabulary, prior understanding and assumptions to revisit during the unit.

Formative assessment

Throughout the lessons, the teacher will use questioning, observation and short checks to guide feedback:

Lesson 1: Check appropriate units, measurement techniques and the distinction between observation and inference.

Lesson 2: Review investigation plans before students proceed, checking variables, feasibility and safe procedures.

Lesson 3: Check data recording, repeat measurements, graph selection and interpretation of unexpected results.

Lesson 4: Use structured peer feedback to improve the clarity of conclusions and the quality of supporting evidence.

Exit tickets will identify misconceptions and inform the next lesson. For example: Can our results show that diameter alone caused the difference in bending? Explain.

Summative assessment

Each student will submit a short investigation report or an equivalent recorded presentation with accompanying data and a graph. It will include:

The investigation question and hypothesis.

The method and identified variables.

Results and an appropriate graph.

A conclusion supported by specific results.

At least two limitations and suggested improvements.

A justified recommendation for the model structure.

Group data may be shared, but each student will provide their own explanation and evaluation.

Differentiation and access

Students who need support will receive vocabulary prompts, partially completed recording tables, graph scaffolds and sentence starters. Oral or recorded explanations can provide an alternative to extended writing while retaining the same scientific expectations.

Extension tasks will ask students to distinguish repeat readings from independent samples, discuss confounding variables and propose a stronger investigation. Assessment will prioritise scientific understanding and evidence-based reasoning.

Lesson 1 Observe and compare

Learning objectives

Describe at least four characteristics of stick samples using appropriate vocabulary.

Measure length, diameter and mass, recording values with suitable units.

Distinguish direct observations from inferences.

Resources and preparation

Provide labelled, inspected stick samples; rulers; balances; callipers where available; magnifiers; recording sheets; and vocabulary cards. Remove sharp, splintered or visibly mouldy samples. Use pre-collected materials to ensure accessibility and avoid disturbing habitats.

AI integration

[AI PREPARATION] Generate a vocabulary sheet and two versions of the recording sheet: one with prompts and one with fewer scaffolds. Check definitions, units and reading level.

Differentiation strategies

Support: illustrated vocabulary, labelled equipment, a worked recording example and sentence starters.

Access: stabilise samples on trays; offer a recording or discussion role where handling is difficult.

Extension: compare diameter at several positions and explain why irregularity affects comparisons.

Success criteria

Students record at least four relevant characteristics, use suitable units and correctly distinguish observation from inference.

Lesson 2 Predict and plan

Learning objectives

Write a testable hypothesis about stick diameter and bending.

Identify the independent variable, dependent variable and at least three control variables.

Produce a workable investigation plan and explain two limitations.

Resources and preparation

Provide Lesson 1 results, planning templates, inspected samples, stable supports, rulers and a small load. The teacher must pilot the setup beforehand to select a common support spacing and load that produce measurable bending without damage. If this is not feasible, revise the samples or setup before student testing.

AI integration

[AI PREPARATION] Generate contrasting investigation plans containing deliberate methodological weaknesses. Check that the weaknesses are clear and appropriate for students to evaluate.

Differentiation strategies

Support: variable-sorting cards, a partially completed method and an apparatus diagram.

Access: written and spoken instructions; planning roles that allow different forms of contribution.

Extension: distinguish repeated readings on one stick from additional independent sticks. Explain why natural samples limit causal claims.

Success criteria

The plan identifies diameter as the comparison variable, midpoint displacement as the measured outcome, and consistent load, support spacing and measurement procedure.

Lesson 3 Investigate and analyse

Learning objectives

Follow the approved method and record repeat measurements.

Calculate mean displacement where repeat readings are valid.

Plot a scatter graph and describe the relationship shown by the data.

Resources and preparation

Provide approved plans, labelled sticks, stable supports, the teacher-selected load, rulers, recording sheets, calculators and graph paper or spreadsheet software. Keep loads close to the bench surface and secure supports against movement. Use appropriate eye protection under the school’s risk assessment. Do not test to failure. Stop if a sample cracks, slips or retains visible bending.

AI integration

[AI PREPARATION] Prepare a graph checklist and differentiated calculation prompts. Any example dataset must be labelled simulated practice data and kept separate from experimental results.

Differentiation strategies

Support: results table with units, a worked mean calculation and graph axes with suggested scales.

Access: flexible practical roles and accessible apparatus positioning.

Extension: calculate ranges, discuss measurement resolution and explain why repeat readings do not remove differences between natural samples.

Success criteria

Students record traceable results, produce an appropriate graph and describe the pattern accurately, including where evidence is inconclusive.

Lesson 4 Evaluate and apply

Learning objectives

Write a conclusion supported by at least two specific results.

Explain at least two limitations and propose linked improvements.

Recommend suitable sticks for a model structure while recognising the limits of the evidence.

Resources and preparation

Provide students’ results and graphs, the assessment rubric, report templates and the design brief below. The teacher sets a span consistent with the investigation. The task is a recommendation, not a claim that the bridge’s load capacity has been established.

Design brief

Recommend sticks for a small model bridge with a specified span. Use the investigation to justify your choice of members that should resist bending, and identify further tests needed before construction.

AI integration

[AI PREPARATION] Draft two sample conclusions: one that overstates the findings and one that uses evidence cautiously. Check both against the scientific expectations.

Differentiation strategies

Support: report headings, sentence starters and prompts directing students to specific results.

Access: recorded explanation with the same required data, graph and evaluation.

Extension: propose a stronger sampling strategy and explain further tests to distinguish stiffness, breaking strength and joint performance.

Success criteria

Recommendations use measured evidence, limitations have practical improvements, and conclusions stay within what was tested.

A stick is a natural material with characteristics that may vary along its length. Measure carefully and describe what you observe before deciding what it means.

TermMeaning and example

Calculation support

Mean = sum of valid movement readings ÷ number of valid readings. Simulated example only: (4 + 5 + 3) ÷ 3 = 4 mm. Do not copy these figures into your experimental results.

Record unexpected results, procedural changes or invalid readings and explain any exclusions. Do not remove a result simply because it does not fit the prediction.

Practical checklist

Keep hands and faces clear. Follow the approved setup.

Stop for cracking, slipping or permanent bending and tell the teacher.

Use only valid repeat readings to calculate the mean.

Laboratory preparation and scientific model

The tutor pilots the apparatus, determines safe load increments and a measurable displacement range, and approves the protocol. Use stable rounded supports, a centred loading attachment, a displacement gauge or suitable measuring system, callipers, a balance and inspected specimens. Record loading hardware mass. Do not prescribe universal loads for variable natural samples.

Use non-destructive loading only; stop for cracking, slipping, residual deformation or departure from the approved range.

Keep loads close to the bench, retain falling loads where appropriate and keep hands and faces clear. Follow local laboratory risk assessment and eye-protection requirements.

Select reasonably straight specimens and document species if verified, visible defects, bark, curvature and conditioning. Unknown species or moisture must be recorded as unknown.

Where feasible use at least three independent specimens per comparison group. If availability prevents this, limit claims to tested specimens rather than making population inferences.

Agree a fixed span, orientation and reading time. Record several cross-sections and orthogonal diameters; do not hide taper or ovality inside one unqualified diameter.

Model handout

For a simply supported, slender, prismatic beam with a central point force, small deflection and linear elastic behaviour:

δ = F L³ / (48 E I) k = dF/dδ E_app = k L³ / (48 I)

For a solid circular section: I = π d⁴ / 64. Here δ is midpoint displacement (m), F is applied force (N), L is support span (m), E is modulus (Pa), I is second moment of area (m⁴), and k is specimen stiffness (N/m). Use F = mg, with g = 9.81 m/s² and total added mass in kg.

These equations are an idealisation. Natural sticks may be tapered, curved, anisotropic, heterogeneous or damaged. Support indentation, slip, shear effects, loading history and creep can affect readings. E_app is an apparent estimate under the adopted model, not automatically a species-level material constant. If geometry is not adequately represented, report k and model limitations instead of forcing a modulus estimate.

Source: University of Cambridge, The Three Point Bend Test, theory note. https://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/IALego/bender_files/bend_theory.pdf

Diagnostic and activity answers

0.40 mm = 0.00040 m. Slope of F against δ is N/m when SI units are used. Repeated cycles describe one specimen’s response and may be affected by loading history; independent specimens are needed to assess between-specimen variation. High R² measures fit to the chosen relationship, not truth of its assumptions or strength. Diameter uncertainty enters the approximate relative modulus uncertainty with coefficient 4.

Ideal-model scaling: doubling span increases displacement eightfold at constant F, E and I; doubling circular diameter reduces displacement sixteenfold at constant F, E and L. Real specimens may violate geometry, small-deflection, support or material assumptions.

Characterisation precedes protocol design; piloting precedes data collection; analysis precedes application. This sequencing exposes the dependence of conclusions on measurement, sampling and physical assumptions. The extended two-hour sessions provide time for apparatus checks, independent thinking and critique, but actual timing must be trialled.

Scaffolded calculations and open analysis routes accommodate varied preparation without lowering the outcomes. Group practical work supports shared equipment use; individual reports and oral checks provide evidence of personal understanding. Practical access and mathematical confidence should be reviewed after each session, with scaffolds adjusted in response.

Tool and model evaluation

Generative AI supports drafting, alternative explanations and critique examples. It does not validate theory, references, student achievement or safe load limits. A spreadsheet or notebook makes numerical analysis inspectable; a tutor-verified worked example checks the workflow. No controlled comparison of AI models, preparation-time study or classroom outcome evaluation has been conducted. Any effectiveness claim remains provisional.

This portfolio demonstrates that collaborative process; it does not claim independently authored code or a comparison of AI model performance.

How I would evaluate effectiveness

I would compare matched entry and exit responses, inspect individual work against the rubric, review participation and accessibility feedback, and check practical timings and data quality. Before classroom deployment I would verify content and calculations, test accessibility and browser behaviour, pilot the apparatus and workload, and align assessment to local requirements. Multi-device collection and reliable AI-supported written feedback would be separate development stages, with teacher oversight.

How the learning changes in practice

Example 1 From a fair comparison to a defensible model

For a 15-year-old, I ask: How does stick diameter relate to bending under the same load? Learners identify variables, repeat readings and explain why moisture or condition could affect their conclusion. The focus is a fair comparison and a reasoned account of the pattern.

For an undergraduate, I ask: What can force–deflection measurements tell us about specimen stiffness, and when is an apparent modulus defensible? Learners justify sampling, inspect the fitted relationship and evaluate geometry and model assumptions. The focus moves to the validity and limits of a quantitative explanation.

Example 2 From support prompts to justified decisions

The school worksheet helps learners organise observations and conclusions through vocabulary, recording tables and sentence starters. The undergraduate worksheet requires a traceable protocol, dimensional consistency and a rationale for analysis decisions. Both provide access, while the expected reasoning changes.

Example 3 From checking an answer to interpreting evidence

The age 15 interactive activity checks means and comparisons, then asks learners to challenge the statement that the thickest stick is always strongest. The undergraduate activity checks stiffness and apparent modulus calculations, then asks whether a strong statistical fit validates the physical model. Each interaction targets a misconception appropriate to its learning outcomes.

My design judgement and evaluation approach

What stays consistent

Both versions retain a clear enquiry sequence, accessible resources, individual evidence of understanding and conclusions bounded by the data. I preserve these principles while changing the mathematical demand, degree of independence and assessment standard. Practical piloting and teacher-approved procedures remain essential at both levels.

What AI contributes

I used generative AI collaboratively to develop and iterate planning documents, differentiated activities, assessment prompts and prototype interfaces. I directed the audience changes and requested revisions to interaction, submission flow and teacher reporting. The technical implementation was AI-assisted. The portfolio makes the use of AI visible rather than presenting generated material as independently verified teaching evidence.

My intended workflow includes checking content, calculations, accessibility and alignment before delivery, then revising resources using actual learner evidence. The prototypes demonstrate features and scoring logic; classroom usability and impact still require piloting. A specific AI model comparison has not been conducted.
